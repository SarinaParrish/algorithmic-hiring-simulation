# Evidence Package C
# Bias Amplification and Feedback Loops in Algorithmic Decision-Making

## Purpose

This evidence package examines how small individual decision biases can accumulate into larger systemic disparities over time. It provides the theoretical foundation for Experiment 3 (Culture-Fit Feedback Loop) and supports the broader research question of how repeated personality-weighting strategies may influence workforce composition.

Unlike Package B, which focuses on the existence and use of algorithmic hiring systems, this package focuses on **dynamic behavior**—how repeated algorithmic decisions may reinforce previous outcomes through feedback loops.

Supports:
- Experiment 2 – Personality-Weighted Hiring
- Experiment 3 – Culture-Fit Feedback Loop

---

# Executive Summary

One of the central concerns in algorithmic decision-making is that seemingly small preferences or biases can become amplified when decisions are repeated over time. Rather than existing as isolated events, hiring decisions influence future applicant pools, organizational culture, workforce composition, and subsequent hiring criteria.

Researchers describe this phenomenon using terms such as cumulative disadvantage, feedback loops, algorithmic bias amplification, and emergent disparities. These mechanisms do not necessarily require intentionally discriminatory algorithms. Instead, repeated optimization toward historical preferences or organizational objectives may gradually reinforce existing patterns.

This concept directly supports the capstone's simulation design. Rather than attempting to predict how any specific hiring algorithm behaves, the simulation models one plausible mechanism through which repeated personality-weighting strategies may alter workforce composition over multiple hiring rounds.

Current research generally agrees that algorithmic systems can reinforce existing patterns if feedback mechanisms are left unchecked. However, researchers continue to debate the magnitude of these effects, the best methods for measuring fairness, and which interventions are most effective.

---

# Key Takeaways

- Small algorithmic preferences can accumulate through repeated decision cycles.
- Feedback loops may reinforce existing workforce characteristics.
- Bias amplification does not require intentionally discriminatory algorithms.
- Emergent disparities can result from repeated optimization toward historical patterns.
- Measuring fairness requires examining long-term system behavior, not only individual decisions.
- The simulation models one possible amplification mechanism rather than proving real-world outcomes.

---

# Current Research Consensus

## Strong Evidence

- Algorithmic systems can reinforce existing patterns through repeated feedback.
- Small differences in early decisions may produce larger downstream effects.
- Long-term outcomes should be evaluated in addition to individual hiring decisions.
- Historical training data can contribute to future disparities.

## Mixed / Ongoing Debate

- How large amplification effects become in practice.
- Which fairness metrics best capture long-term bias.
- Whether feedback loops are primarily caused by data, algorithms, or organizational practices.
- Which interventions most effectively reduce amplification.

# Major Sources

## Selbst et al. (2019)

**Why it Matters**

Foundational paper explaining why fairness problems become more complex when algorithms operate inside larger social systems.

**Key Findings**

- Fairness cannot be evaluated in isolation.
- Algorithmic systems interact with organizational and social environments.
- Narrow technical fixes often overlook broader systemic effects.

**Supports**

Experiment 3

**Limitations**

Conceptual paper rather than empirical hiring study.

## Liu et al. (2018)

**Why it Matters**

Classic work demonstrating delayed-impact and feedback effects in algorithmic decision-making.

**Key Findings**

- Fair decisions today may produce unintended long-term consequences.
- Feedback effects change future populations.
- Static fairness metrics may overlook dynamic outcomes.

**Supports**

Experiment 3

**Limitations**

Not specific to hiring.

## Danks & London (2017)

**Why it Matters**

Introduces the concept of algorithmic bias within complex sociotechnical systems.

**Key Findings**

- Bias emerges from interactions between algorithms and human institutions.
- Evaluation requires considering the broader decision system.
- Algorithmic fairness is not purely a technical problem.

**Supports**

Experiments 2 and 3

**Limitations**

Broad AI ethics paper.

## Hooker (2021)

**Why it Matters**

# Living Claim Map

## Supported Claims

- Repeated algorithmic decisions can reinforce earlier outcomes.
- Feedback loops are an important concern in AI systems.
- Long-term system behavior matters when evaluating fairness.
- Emergent disparities may arise without intentional discrimination.
- Monitoring algorithmic systems over time is important.

## Mixed Claims

- Bias amplification always occurs.
- Feedback loops inevitably reduce diversity.
- Existing fairness metrics adequately capture long-term effects.
- Bias amplification is caused primarily by algorithms rather than organizational processes.

## Claims the Literature Cannot Make

- Every AI hiring system develops harmful feedback loops.
- All algorithmic hiring systems amplify bias.
- One fairness metric captures every relevant outcome.

## Claims My Simulation Cannot Make

- The simulation cannot estimate the magnitude of real-world bias amplification.
- The simulation cannot prove that commercial hiring systems create feedback loops.
- The simulation cannot predict workforce changes within actual organizations.
- The simulation models one plausible amplification mechanism rather than validating a specific hiring algorithm.

The defensible conclusion is:

Repeated personality-weighting strategies may contribute to changing workforce composition over time under certain modeled conditions. The simulation illustrates one possible mechanism rather than proving how real hiring systems behave.
