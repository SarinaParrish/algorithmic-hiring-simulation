# Evidence Package A
# Big Five Personality Assessment in Hiring

## Purpose

This evidence package examines how Big Five personality traits are used and studied in employment selection. It supports Experiment 2 — Personality-Weighted Hiring, where traits such as conscientiousness and extraversion receive higher weights than the baseline condition.

This package does not argue that personality testing is always accurate, fair, or appropriate. Its purpose is to explain why personality weighting is a realistic experimental condition and what limits should be acknowledged when interpreting the simulation.

---

## Executive Summary

Research in personnel psychology has long studied whether personality traits predict workplace performance. The Big Five framework — openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and emotional stability/neuroticism — is one of the most widely used models for organizing personality research.

The strongest and most consistent finding is that conscientiousness is associated with job performance across many roles. Barrick and Mount’s 1991 meta-analysis is especially important because it found conscientiousness to be the Big Five trait with the broadest relationship to job performance. This supports the design choice in Experiment 2 to test what happens when conscientiousness receives greater weight in a hiring algorithm. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

The evidence for extraversion is more context-dependent. Extraversion appears more relevant in jobs involving social interaction, persuasion, leadership, sales, or training. This means extraversion can be included as a plausible weighted trait, but the simulation should not imply that extraversion is universally desirable.

Personality assessment should be treated as one hiring input among many. Schmidt and Hunter’s work places personality-related measures within a broader selection system that can also include cognitive ability, structured interviews, work samples, job knowledge, and other predictors. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

More recent research also cautions that older validity estimates in personnel selection should be interpreted carefully. Sackett et al. revisit prior validity estimates and argue that some estimates were affected by methodological issues involving range-restriction corrections. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Overall, this evidence package supports the idea that personality-weighted hiring is a realistic and research-informed simulation condition. However, it does not support claiming that personality traits alone determine candidate quality, that one weighting strategy is universally best, or that the simulation proves real-world hiring discrimination.

## Key Takeaways

- Big Five personality traits are widely used in workplace and personnel-selection research.
- Conscientiousness has the strongest support as a general predictor of job performance.
- Extraversion is more role-dependent and should be interpreted cautiously.
- Personality assessment can contribute to hiring decisions but should not be treated as the entire decision system.
- Experiment 2 is research-informed because organizations may plausibly weight certain personality traits more heavily.
- The simulation cannot prove that real hiring algorithms use these exact weights or that personality-weighted hiring is always unfair.

## Current Research Consensus

### Strong Evidence

- The Big Five is a major framework for studying personality in organizational psychology.
- Conscientiousness is the most consistently supported Big Five predictor of job performance.
- Personality measures are used in personnel selection and have been studied as part of hiring systems.
- Personality traits should generally be combined with other selection methods rather than used alone.

### Mixed / Ongoing Debate

- How large the predictive value of personality traits actually is.
- Whether personality tests meaningfully improve hiring decisions in every context.
- How much applicant faking or response distortion affects test validity.
- Whether older personnel-selection validity estimates were too high.
- How personality assessment changes when implemented through AI-assisted hiring systems.

## Major Sources

### Barrick & Mount (1991)

**Why it Matters**  
Landmark meta-analysis connecting the Big Five to job performance. This is the primary justification for weighting conscientiousness in Experiment 2.

**Key Findings**
- Conscientiousness showed the strongest general relationship with job performance.
- Other traits, including extraversion, were more context-dependent.
- The Big Five framework helps organize personality-performance research.

**Supports**  
Experiment 2

**Limitations**  
Does not study AI hiring, algorithmic scoring, or feedback loops.

---

### Schmidt & Hunter (1998)

**Why it Matters**  
Places personality measures within the larger field of personnel selection. Useful for explaining that personality should be one input among many.

**Key Findings**
- Reviewed decades of evidence on selection methods.
- Compared personality-related measures with interviews, work samples, job knowledge, and cognitive ability.
- Shows why structured hiring tools may use measurable predictors.

**Supports**  
Experiment 1 and Experiment 2

**Limitations**  
Does not justify using personality as the sole hiring criterion.

---

### Rothstein & Goffin (2006)

**Why it Matters**  
Directly reviews personality measures in personnel selection and explains why they became more common in hiring contexts.

**Key Findings**
- Personality assessment has been used in employment selection.
- Research and practice have increased interest in personality-based screening.
- The usefulness of personality testing depends on context and implementation.

**Supports**  
Experiment 2

**Limitations**  
Published before the current wave of AI-assisted hiring tools.

---

### Sackett et al. (2022)

**Why it Matters**  
Important caution source. Helps prevent overstating older personnel-selection findings.

**Key Findings**
- Revisits prior meta-analytic validity estimates.
- Raises concerns about overcorrection for range restriction.
- Shows that personnel-selection validity estimates require careful interpretation.

**Supports**  
Experiment 1 and Experiment 2

**Limitations**  
Not focused only on Big Five personality traits.

## Connection to the Simulation

### Research Supports

- Using Big Five traits as a plausible basis for personality scoring.
- Treating conscientiousness as a reasonable trait to weight more heavily in Experiment 2.
- Testing extraversion as a weighted trait, especially as a role- or culture-dependent preference.
- Comparing balanced trait weighting against personality-weighted scoring.

### Research Does Not Support

- Using personality as the only hiring criterion.
- Claiming conscientiousness or extraversion always produce better employees.
- Claiming one personality weighting strategy is universally correct.
- Claiming that real companies use the same exact scoring weights as the simulation.

## Living Claim Map

### Supported Claims

- Big Five personality traits are widely studied in workplace and personnel-selection research.
- Conscientiousness is one of the strongest and most consistent personality predictors of job performance.
- Extraversion may be relevant in some job contexts but is less universal than conscientiousness.
- Personality assessments are used in personnel-selection contexts.
- Personality should be treated as one component of hiring, not the whole system.

### Mixed Claims

- Personality testing improves hiring outcomes.
- Extraversion is a desirable hiring trait.
- Personality assessments are objective.
- Classic validity estimates fully capture the predictive value of selection methods.
- Personality-weighted hiring is more effective than balanced hiring.

### Claims the Literature Cannot Make

- Conscientiousness always predicts the best employee.
- Extraversion is always beneficial in hiring.
- Personality testing is always fair.
- Personality assessments should replace interviews, work samples, experience, or skill-based evaluation.

### Claims My Simulation Cannot Make

- The simulation cannot prove that real hiring algorithms discriminate.
- The simulation cannot prove that conscientiousness or extraversion cause better job performance.
- The simulation cannot claim that companies use the exact trait weights modeled.
- The simulation cannot predict real workforce composition outcomes.
- The simulation cannot determine whether personality testing should or should not be used in hiring.

The defensible claim is: this simulation models one plausible mechanism by which personality-weighting choices may affect workforce composition over repeated hiring rounds.
