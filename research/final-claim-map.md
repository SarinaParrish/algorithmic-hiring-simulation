# Final Claim Map
# Modeling Personality-Based Bias in Algorithmic Hiring

## Purpose

This document consolidates the Living Claim Maps from all evidence packages into one reference.

Its purpose is to distinguish:

- Claims strongly supported by existing research
- Claims that remain debated or context-dependent
- Claims that cannot be supported by the current literature
- Claims that cannot be made by this simulation

This document serves as a guide for writing the final capstone paper, interpreting simulation results responsibly, and avoiding unsupported conclusions.

---

# Supported Claims

The following claims are well supported by current research.

## Personality Assessment

- The Big Five is one of the most widely studied personality frameworks.
- Conscientiousness is one of the strongest and most consistent personality predictors of job performance.
- Extraversion may be relevant in certain occupations but is more context-dependent.
- Personality assessments are used in some employment selection processes.
- Personality should generally be considered alongside other hiring criteria rather than used alone.

(Evidence Package A)

---

## Algorithmic Hiring

- Organizations increasingly use AI-assisted hiring systems.
- Algorithmic systems can automate candidate screening, scoring, and ranking.
- Hiring algorithms should be evaluated and monitored rather than assumed to be neutral.
- Algorithmic decision-making may introduce or reproduce bias depending on system design.

(Evidence Package B)

---

## Bias Amplification

- Small decision differences can accumulate over repeated decision cycles.
- Feedback loops may reinforce existing organizational patterns.
- Long-term system behavior should be considered when evaluating fairness.
- Emergent disparities do not necessarily require intentionally discriminatory algorithms.

(Evidence Package C)

---

## Culture Fit

- Hiring decisions may include judgments about organizational or cultural fit.
- Similarity-based hiring can reinforce existing workforce characteristics.
- Homophily is a well-established mechanism in organizational and social research.
- Organizational composition may influence future hiring preferences.

(Evidence Package D)

---

## Agent-Based Modeling

- Agent-based modeling is an accepted research methodology.
- Simulations are appropriate for studying complex adaptive systems.
- Emergent behavior can arise from repeated local decision rules.
- Simulations are useful for exploring hypothetical scenarios that cannot easily be tested in practice.

(Evidence Package E)

---

## Responsible AI

- Automated hiring systems require governance, evaluation, and monitoring.
- Bias audits and transparency are increasingly emphasized in AI governance.
- Responsible AI requires ongoing risk management rather than one-time evaluation.
- Employment-related AI systems are increasingly viewed as high-impact applications.

(Evidence Package F)

---

# Mixed / Nuanced Claims

The following claims are discussed in the literature but remain context-dependent.

- Personality testing consistently improves hiring outcomes.
- Extraversion should be prioritized in hiring.
- AI hiring systems reduce bias compared to human decision-makers.
- AI hiring systems increase workforce diversity.
- Culture fit is always harmful.
- Culture-add hiring eliminates similarity bias.
- Existing fairness metrics fully capture algorithmic bias.
- Current bias audit laws are sufficient.
- Human oversight alone prevents algorithmic harm.
- Agent-based simulations accurately predict real organizations.

These claims should be discussed cautiously and presented with appropriate limitations.

---

# Claims the Literature Cannot Make

The current body of research does **not** support the following conclusions.

- One personality profile defines the ideal employee.
- Personality assessments should replace other hiring methods.
- All AI hiring systems behave similarly.
- Every hiring algorithm produces bias amplification.
- Every organization hires primarily for culture fit.
- Every feedback loop produces discrimination.
- Every bias audit guarantees fairness.
- One fairness metric captures every important outcome.

---

# Claims My Simulation Cannot Make

The simulation was intentionally designed as an exploratory model rather than a predictive system.

It **cannot** claim:

- That real companies use the same personality weights.
- That commercial hiring algorithms operate like this model.
- That the simulation predicts actual workforce demographics.
- That personality weighting causes real-world discrimination.
- That culture-fit hiring necessarily produces demographic disparities.
- That the simulation functions as a legal bias audit.
- That any employer violates employment law.
- That one hiring strategy is universally superior.

---

# Defensible Conclusions

The following conclusions are supported by both the literature and the implemented simulation.

- Different personality-weighting strategies can produce different simulated workforce outcomes.
- Repeated decision rules may generate emergent organizational patterns over time.
- Culture-fit feedback mechanisms may reinforce existing workforce composition under certain modeled conditions.
- Agent-based simulation provides an appropriate methodology for exploring these questions.
- Simulation results should be interpreted as plausible mechanisms rather than predictions of real organizational behavior.
- Responsible evaluation of AI hiring systems requires attention to fairness, transparency, and long-term outcomes.

---

# Final Research Position

This capstone does **not** attempt to determine whether AI hiring systems are inherently fair or unfair.

Instead, it uses an agent-based simulation to model one plausible mechanism through which different personality-weighting strategies and feedback processes may influence workforce composition over repeated hiring rounds.

The findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory, research-informed, and hypothesis-generating rather than predictive or prescriptive.
