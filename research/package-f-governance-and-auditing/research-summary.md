# Evidence Package F
# Governance, Auditing, and Responsible AI in Automated Hiring

## Purpose

This evidence package examines governance, auditing, and responsible AI frameworks related to automated hiring systems. It supports the final discussion and limitations section of the capstone by explaining why algorithmic hiring systems require evaluation, documentation, monitoring, and accountability.

Unlike Packages A–E, this package is less about designing the simulation and more about interpreting its relevance. The goal is to connect the capstone to real-world debates about automated employment decision tools, bias audits, fairness, transparency, and AI risk management.

Supports:
- Final Discussion
- Limitations
- Responsible AI Framing
- Experiments 1–3

---

## Executive Summary

Automated hiring systems are increasingly treated as high-impact AI systems because they can influence access to employment opportunities. As a result, researchers, regulators, and public agencies have focused on how these tools should be evaluated, audited, documented, and governed.

New York City Local Law 144 is one of the most relevant U.S. examples for this capstone. It restricts employers and employment agencies from using automated employment decision tools unless the tool has received a recent bias audit, audit information is publicly available, and candidates or employees receive required notices. This shows that automated hiring tools are not only a technical issue, but also a governance and accountability issue.

The EEOC has also identified algorithmic fairness in employment as an enforcement and guidance priority. Its Artificial Intelligence and Algorithmic Fairness Initiative focuses on employment-related AI tools, their design, adoption, and potential impact under federal equal employment opportunity laws.

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework provides a broader responsible AI lens. It emphasizes that AI risk management should include governance, mapping, measurement, and management. For this capstone, NIST is useful because it supports the idea that AI systems should be evaluated over time rather than assumed to be neutral after deployment.

The EU AI Act is also relevant because it classifies AI systems used for recruitment, application filtering, and candidate evaluation as high-risk systems. Even though the capstone is U.S.-based, this international example shows that employment-related AI is increasingly viewed as a domain requiring special oversight.

Overall, this package supports the final argument that automated hiring systems require careful evaluation because small scoring choices, repeated over time, may affect workforce outcomes. However, it does not mean that the simulation is itself a legal audit or that it can determine whether a real hiring system complies with law.

## Key Takeaways

- Automated hiring systems are increasingly subject to legal, regulatory, and governance scrutiny.
- NYC Local Law 144 requires bias audits, public audit summaries, and candidate notices for covered automated employment decision tools.
- The EEOC treats AI and algorithmic fairness in employment as an important equal employment opportunity issue.
- NIST frames responsible AI as an ongoing risk management process.
- The EU AI Act treats recruitment and candidate evaluation AI systems as high-risk.
- The capstone can inform responsible discussion, but it is not a compliance audit or legal analysis.

## Current Research Consensus

### Strong Evidence

- Automated employment systems can have significant effects on opportunity access.
- Hiring algorithms should be evaluated for fairness, validity, and impact.
- Documentation, transparency, and monitoring are important for responsible AI deployment.
- Bias audits are becoming part of the governance landscape for automated hiring.
- Human oversight and accountability matter when AI tools influence employment decisions.

### Mixed / Ongoing Debate

- What counts as a sufficient bias audit.
- Which fairness metrics are most appropriate for hiring.
- Whether current audit laws are strong enough.
- How transparent vendors should be about proprietary models.
- Whether regulation should focus on vendors, employers, or both.
- Whether bias audits capture long-term feedback effects.

## Major Sources

### NYC Local Law 144

**Why it Matters**  
Most directly relevant U.S. regulation for automated employment decision tools. It shows that hiring algorithms are already being regulated through bias audit and notice requirements.

**Key Findings**
- Covered automated employment decision tools must undergo a bias audit within one year of use.
- Employers must make audit information publicly available.
- Candidates and employees must receive required notices.

**Supports**  
Final Discussion and Responsible AI Framing

**Limitations**  
Applies specifically to covered tools and employers under NYC law; not a universal national standard.

---

### EEOC Artificial Intelligence and Algorithmic Fairness Initiative

**Why it Matters**  
Shows that U.S. employment regulators are paying attention to AI tools used in hiring and employment decisions.

**Key Findings**
- EEOC launched an initiative focused on AI and algorithmic fairness.
- The initiative includes technical assistance, stakeholder listening, and information gathering.
- AI employment tools are considered in relation to federal equal employment opportunity laws.

**Supports**  
Final Discussion and Limitations

**Limitations**  
Guidance and enforcement framing, not a technical model or simulation study.

---

### NIST AI Risk Management Framework

**Why it Matters**  
Provides a widely cited responsible AI framework that can be used to frame evaluation, monitoring, and documentation.

**Key Findings**
- AI risk management should include govern, map, measure, and manage functions.
- Trustworthy AI includes validity, reliability, accountability, transparency, explainability, privacy, and fairness.
- AI risk management is ongoing and context-dependent.

**Supports**  
All experiments and final discussion

**Limitations**  
General AI framework, not specific to hiring.

---

### EU AI Act

**Why it Matters**  
Important international example showing that employment-related AI systems are treated as high-risk.

**Key Findings**
- AI systems used for recruitment or selection may be classified as high-risk.
- This includes systems used to place targeted job ads, filter applications, and evaluate candidates.
- High-risk classification reflects concern about effects on fundamental rights and access to employment.

**Supports**  
Final Discussion and Responsible AI Framing

**Limitations**  
European law; not directly applicable to a U.S. capstone unless discussed as international context.

---

### Bogen & Rieke (2018)

**Why it Matters**  
Useful bridge between policy and hiring technology. Explains how hiring algorithms can affect opportunity access and why transparency matters.

**Key Findings**
- Automated hiring tools can influence who is recruited, screened, ranked, or advanced.
- Bias may enter through data, design, and proxy variables.
- Accountability and transparency are important when tools affect employment opportunity.

**Supports**  
Final Discussion and Responsible AI Framing

**Limitations**  
Policy report rather than peer-reviewed empirical study.

## Connection to the Simulation

### Research Supports

- Discussing automated hiring as a high-impact AI use case.
- Explaining why scoring rules should be evaluated and documented.
- Using simulation logs and visualizations as a responsible analysis practice.
- Comparing outcomes across multiple experimental conditions.
- Framing repeated hiring outcomes as part of system-level risk.
- Including limitations and avoiding overclaiming.

### Research Does Not Support

- Treating the simulation as a legal compliance audit.
- Claiming the simulation satisfies bias audit requirements.
- Claiming the simulation determines whether any real tool violates employment law.
- Claiming governance frameworks prove the model is correct.
- Treating regulation as settled or uniform across jurisdictions.

## Living Claim Map

### Supported Claims

- Automated hiring systems are subject to increasing governance attention.
- Bias audits, documentation, and notices are emerging requirements in some jurisdictions.
- Employment-related AI can affect access to opportunity.
- Responsible AI frameworks emphasize ongoing monitoring and evaluation.
- Automated hiring systems should not be assumed neutral simply because they are technical.

### Mixed Claims

- Current bias audit laws are sufficient.
- Bias audits fully capture algorithmic fairness.
- Human oversight prevents algorithmic harm.
- Existing regulation keeps pace with AI hiring technology.
- One governance framework can address all hiring risks.

### Claims the Literature Cannot Make

- Every automated hiring system is illegal or discriminatory.
- Every bias audit is effective.
- Regulation guarantees fair outcomes.
- All jurisdictions govern AI hiring the same way.
- Compliance equals ethical or fair deployment.

### Claims My Simulation Cannot Make

- The simulation cannot determine legal compliance.
- The simulation cannot function as a real-world bias audit.
- The simulation cannot prove a vendor tool is fair or unfair.
- The simulation cannot replace validation using real applicant and hiring data.
- The simulation cannot determine whether an employer violated employment law.

The defensible conclusion is:

This simulation can support responsible discussion about why automated hiring systems should be evaluated over time, but it should be treated as an exploratory model rather than a legal, regulatory, or compliance audit.
